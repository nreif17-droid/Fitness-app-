# PART 0 — SLEEP SCIENCE

The training library opens its non-negotiable layer with *"Sleep 7–9 h. This is the
highest-leverage variable in the document. Nothing below matters if this is broken."*
That claim is correct and this file is the substance behind it.

---

## Why sleep outranks almost everything else

Sleep is where the adaptation the training actually produces gets consolidated:

- **Growth hormone** is released predominantly during slow-wave (deep) sleep
- **Muscle protein synthesis** and tissue repair are elevated during sleep
- **Glycogen resynthesis** continues overnight
- **Motor learning consolidates** during sleep — this is why skill-heavy modalities
  (Olympic lifting, swimming, calisthenics) are disproportionately hurt by poor sleep
- **Immune function** depends on it; under-slept athletes get sick more, and illness
  costs more training days than almost any other interruption
- **Injury risk rises measurably** with chronic short sleep, particularly in
  adolescents

---

## How much

| Population | Target |
|---|---|
| General adult | 7–9 h |
| Athlete in a training block | 8–10 h |
| High-volume endurance / heavy training load | 9–10 h, sometimes more |
| Adolescent athlete | 9–11 h |

Endurance athletes at high volume often require **eight to ten hours**, and elite
athletes frequently need **9–10 h** to support the physiological demand. The training
library's "7–9 h" is a general-population floor; **for a user in Week 10 of an advanced
block, 7 h is under-recovery, not adequacy.** The coach should scale the sleep
recommendation with training load rather than quoting one number to everyone.

## Sleep extension — the evidence, honestly

**The famous result:** Stanford basketball players who extended to ~10 h/night for
several weeks improved sprint times, reaction time, and shooting accuracy. Other
sleep-extension work has shown improved sprint drills (~19.1 s → ~17.6 s), better
hitting accuracy and depth, and improved mood.

**The honest caveat:** unlike sleep *loss* — where the performance decrement is
consistent and well-established — sleep *extension* has **ambiguous and sometimes
contradictory** effects across the literature, attributable to differing methods and
designs, with methodological limitations that constrain how far the findings generalize.

**What this means for the product:** recommend adequate sleep confidently, because the
cost of deficit is well-evidenced. Be more measured about promising performance gains
from extension specifically — "sleeping more will make you faster" is over-claiming.
The defensible framing is that **most people are in deficit, and closing a deficit is
what produces the gain.**

## Sleep debt and recovery

- Sleep debt **accumulates** and is not repaid by a single long night. Several nights of
  extra sleep may be needed to recover from a sustained shortfall.
- **Napping** is a legitimate tool:
  - **20–30 min** — restores alertness and reaction time without sleep inertia
  - **90 min** — a full cycle, useful for genuine debt repayment
  - Avoid the 45–75 min range (wake from deep sleep = grogginess)
  - Keep naps before ~3pm to protect night sleep
- **Weekend catch-up** partially works for alertness but doesn't fully reverse metabolic
  consequences. It's mitigation, not a strategy.

## Sleep hygiene — ranked by actual effect size

Most sleep-hygiene advice is repeated indiscriminately. Ranked honestly:

**High impact:**
1. **Consistent wake time**, seven days a week. Anchors circadian rhythm harder than
   bedtime does. The single most useful behavioral lever.
2. **Morning light exposure**, ideally outdoors within an hour of waking.
3. **Cool, dark, quiet room** (~18°C / 65°F). Core temperature must drop for sleep
   onset.
4. **Caffeine cutoff** — 8–10 h before bed. With a ~5–6 h half-life, a 2pm coffee is
   meaningfully active at 10pm. Most people underestimate this badly.
5. **Alcohol** — reduces sleep latency but suppresses REM and fragments the second half
   of the night. It is a sleep destroyer that feels like a sleep aid.

**Moderate:**
6. Consistent pre-sleep routine; limiting late large meals; managing evening light.

**Low / oversold:**
7. Blue-light glasses specifically (effect is small relative to the behavioral levers);
   most sleep supplements; elaborate tracking.

## Training's effect on sleep

The relationship runs both ways, which the coach should account for:

- **Late high-intensity sessions** (within ~2–3 h of bed) delay sleep onset for many
  people via core temperature and sympathetic arousal. Not universal — individual
  variation is large.
- **Very high training loads** can paradoxically *degrade* sleep quality — a signal of
  overreaching, and one of the earliest.
- **The overreaching triad** already in the training library (loss of appetite +
  disturbed sleep + mood crash together) is a stop-and-reassess signal, not a
  train-through-it signal.
- **Travel and competition** wreck sleep predictably. Any event-prep block should plan
  for it rather than be surprised.
